What to Look For

01 /

Video verification, not just sensors

Because SPD now requires evidence before dispatching, a system built around cameras Ã¢â‚¬â€ not door and motion sensors alone Ã¢â‚¬â€ is the difference between an alarm that actually gets a response and one that just makes noise. An honest scope of what you're protecting

A retail storefront, a small office, and a warehouse with after-hours deliveries all need different coverage. Walk the property with an installer and identify every entry point, blind corner, and high-value area before pricing anything.

Access control for who comes and goes

Key fobs or card readers replace shared keys that are easy to lose or copy, and give you a log of exactly who entered and when Ã¢â‚¬â€ useful for both security and simple accountability among staff.

A monitoring plan that fits your hours

24/7 professional monitoring costs roughly $30 to $100+ per month depending on the services included. Confirm whether that covers video verification specifically, or just traditional sensor monitoring that Seattle police may no longer act on alone.

Seattle-Specific Requirements

✓

A licensed monitoring company

The City of Seattle requires every alarm-monitoring company to hold an annual license and Unique Identifying Number (UIN). SPD will not dispatch to calls from an unlicensed company Ã¢â‚¬â€ confirm your provider's license status directly.

✓

Enhanced call verification

Seattle ordinance requires monitoring companies to make at least two phone calls to verify an alarm before requesting police dispatch. Ask how your provider handles this process and how quickly they move through it.

✓

False alarm fee exposure

Seattle bills $115 for a false burglar alarm and $230 for a false panic or duress alarm once SPD is dispatched and arrives, with a lower $30 fee if the call is canceled before arrival. These fees are billed to the alarm company, which may pass them along to you Ã¢â‚¬â€ ask how that's handled.

✓

A one-time waiver, once it counts

Seattle offers a false alarm fee waiver once every 84 months for businesses that attend an alarm user workshop or switch to private guard dispatch response. Know this option exists before your first false alarm costs you.

Pro tip: Ask a prospective provider directly: "If my alarm triggers, what evidence do you send to Seattle Police, and how fast?" A vague answer means your system may ring plenty of bells without ever getting a real response.

Red Flags to Avoid

✕

No video verification capability

A sensor-only system sold without acknowledging Seattle's verified-response policy is being sold on an outdated assumption about how alarms get answered.

✕

Can't confirm they're licensed with the City

If an installer or monitoring company can't produce their UIN, SPD won't dispatch to their calls at all Ã¢â‚¬â€ this isn't a minor paperwork detail.

A number given over the phone without ever seeing the property tends to change significantly once installation starts. Insist on a walkthrough before signing.

✕

Long lease contracts with no ownership option

Multi-year monitoring contracts that bundle in leased hardware can cost more over time than buying equipment outright and shopping monitoring separately.

✕

No plan for false alarm reduction

A provider who doesn't proactively help you reduce false triggers Ã¢â‚¬â€ proper sensor placement, staff training, exit delays Ã¢â‚¬â€ is setting you up for recurring fees rather than fewer of them.

The Bottom Line

Choosing a commercial security system in Seattle comes down to three decisions in order: scope the property honestly before pricing anything, prioritize video verification given SPD's evidence-based dispatch policy, and confirm your monitoring company is properly licensed with the City.

An alarm that only rings, without evidence a real event occurred, may not get anyone to your door at all. Build the system that actually gets a response.
