Small Business Employee Onboarding Checklist. 15 Steps for a Better First Week

A practical, phase-by-phase checklist covering the prep work before day one, the first day itself, and the first week — the window that shapes whether a new hire actually sticks around.

By Denmaq August 27, 2026 9 min read

A new hire's first week decides more than most small business owners realize. Show up disorganized — no laptop ready, no clear plan, a manager who's "in back-to-back meetings all week" — and you've already planted the seed for an early resignation.

Onboarding doesn't need a big HR department or expensive software to do well. It needs a plan, split across three phases: what happens before the new hire's first day, what happens on the day itself, and what happens across the rest of the first week. Here are 15 concrete steps across those three phases, plus the mistakes that undo them.

01

Before Day One — Steps 1–5

01 /

Get paperwork done early

Send offer letters, tax forms, and direct deposit paperwork before the start date so their actual first hours aren't consumed by admin instead of getting oriented.

02 /

Set up their workspace and accounts in advance

Have their email, logins, laptop, and any building access ready before they arrive. Nothing signals disorganization faster than a new hire waiting on IT for their first two hours.

03 /

Assign a buddy or mentor

Pick someone who isn't their direct manager — a peer they can ask informal, "no dumb question" questions to without feeling like they're bothering their boss.

04 /

Prepare a written first-week schedule

A simple day-by-day outline — who they're meeting, what they're learning, what's expected — removes the anxiety of not knowing what to expect walking in.

05 /

Communicate start-day logistics clearly

Arrival time, where to park, what to wear, and who to ask for at the door — small details that prevent an awkward, uncertain first fifteen minutes.

02

The First Day — Steps 6–10

Step What It Involves Why It Matters
06. Warm welcome Someone greets them at arrival and walks them in — no sitting alone at an empty desk First impressions set the emotional tone for everything that follows
07. Role & goal expectations Walk through the role and rough 30/60/90-day goals Removes ambiguity about what success actually looks like
08. Team introductions Introduce them to the wider team, not just their direct manager Builds a sense of belonging beyond one relationship
09. Essential logistics Cover expense submission, sick-day process, and basic office logistics Small unanswered questions accumulate into real friction fast
10. End-of-day check-in A short conversation asking how the day went and answering questions Gives them a chance to raise concerns before they go home and stew on them
03

The Rest of the First Week — Steps 11–15

✓

11. Daily check-ins

Short daily check-ins through the first week, tapering to weekly after — enough presence to catch issues early without feeling like surveillance.

✓

12. Role-specific training

Pair generic company orientation with training actually tailored to their specific job — the two are not the same thing and neither replaces the other.

✓

13. A first small win

Give them a small, completable task early in the week so they leave Friday having actually contributed something, not just absorbed information.

✓

14. Unwritten culture norms

Explain the things no handbook covers — communication style, meeting etiquette, how decisions actually get made day to day.

✓

15. Collect onboarding feedback

Ask directly at the end of week one what worked and what didn't — then actually use that feedback to improve the process for the next hire.

Pro tip: Treat onboarding as a 90-day process with the first week as its foundation, not a single event that ends Friday afternoon. The goals and check-ins you set in week one should carry forward, not disappear once the "official" onboarding is done.

04

Common Onboarding Mistakes

✕

No workspace ready on day one

A new hire waiting on IT for a laptop or login for their first several hours reads as disorganization, no matter how good the rest of onboarding is.

✕

Dumping paperwork with no context

Handing over a stack of forms without explanation makes an already tedious process feel worse, and increases the odds something gets filled out wrong.

✕

No manager check-in all week

A manager who's "too busy" to check in during a new hire's first week sends a clear signal about how much attention they'll get going forward.

✕

Information overload on day one

Trying to cover everything in the first eight hours guarantees most of it won't be retained. Pace training across the week instead of front-loading it.

✕

Treating onboarding as a one-day event

Onboarding that stops after day one leaves a new hire to figure out the rest alone — exactly when they're least equipped to do so.

05

The Bottom Line

None of these 15 steps require a big budget or dedicated HR software — most are just a matter of planning ahead and following through. What separates businesses with strong first weeks from ones with high early turnover is usually discipline, not resources.

A new hire's first week is the cheapest, highest-leverage investment you'll make in whether they stay past the first ninety days. Treat it that way.
